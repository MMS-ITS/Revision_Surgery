1. The clinical situation in brief

A 59-year-old man had a one-anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB / mini-gastric bypass) in 2017 with the gastrojejunostomy at 155 cm. Nine years later he has the classic triad that drives OAGB revision:

Marginal ulcer
Large, chronic, just distal to the gastrojejunostomy; bled in Sept 2026 (Hb fell to 8.2 g/dL).
Bile reflux
Large volume of bile seen in the pouch — the mechanism most associated with OAGB ulceration and reflux.
Hiatal hernia
Hill grade IV (severe).
Gallstones
Multiple 5–6 mm stones, new since 2025; asymptomatic.
H. pylori
Persistently positive after two eradication courses; eradication not yet confirmed.
Anaemia
Recurrent iron deficiency; cold intolerance; on IV iron.
BMI ≈ 27
Weight is not the problem — protecting nutrition and avoiding further weight loss is a goal.

Where the field broadly agrees: for bile reflux + recurrent marginal ulcer after OAGB, converting to a Roux-en-Y configuration (which diverts bile away from the pouch) is the mainstream durable solution; the gallbladder is usually removed in the same sitting because post-RYGB ERCP is very difficult; and the hiatal hernia should be repaired. The disagreements between the four opinions are about the details that matter most: whether to resect the ulcer, whether to cut the vagus nerve, and how long the Roux (alimentary) limb should be.

The three technical fault lines. (1) Keep vs resect the ulcer — Lee keeps it (speed/safety, less dissection); Usama and Fakhrul resect it (removes the diseased tissue, allows biopsy, lower recurrence risk but a fresh anastomosis to heal). (2) Vagotomy or not — Lee adds truncal vagotomy to cut acid; Fakhrul argues this is the wrong target because the driver is bile, and most current literature treats RYGB diversion (± resection) as the primary fix with vagotomy reserved for refractory cases. (3) Limb length — only Lee and Fakhrul commit to numbers; Fakhrul's longer alimentary limb (75 cm) is more explicitly aimed at keeping bile off the pouch, which is the patient's chief concern.

3. Pros and cons of each proposition

Prof. Wei-Jei Lee — RYGB conversion, keep old anastomosis + circumferential suture + truncal vagotomy

Pros

  • Highest declared track record for this exact procedure (OAGB pioneer since 2000; ~30 identical cases; recent complication rate reported near zero).
  • Lowest-trauma operation: no new anastomosis to leak, no drain/NG, 1–2 day stay, fast recovery — attractive given the patient's anaemia and long travel.
  • Engaged with the patient's detailed questions point-by-point; internally consistent surgical logic (keeps blood supply, 2 cm safety margin, tented suture to avoid stenosis).
  • Addresses bile (RYGB diversion) and acid (vagotomy) — belt-and-braces on ulcer recurrence.

Cons

  • Leaving a large, chronically ulcerated, previously-bled anastomosis in situ is the most debated choice — no tissue diagnosis, and "circumferential suturing" of an ulcer is a non-standard, hard-to-verify manoeuvre.
  • Refuses biopsy of a chronic, bled ulcer in a 59-year-old — most guidelines would biopsy to exclude malignancy.
  • Truncal vagotomy adds risk of gastric stasis/dumping; its necessity is contested once bile is diverted (see Fakhrul).
  • Downplays H. pylori and skips structured nutritional/anaesthetic optimisation despite recent bleed, anaemia and complex drug list.
  • 40 cm limb is shorter than many published anti-bile-reflux conversions.

Dr Usama Iqbal — RYGB conversion with resection of the ulcerated anastomosis + new anastomosis, all-in-one

Pros

  • Resecting the diseased anastomosis removes the ulcer-bearing tissue outright — the most definitive answer to a chronic, bled ulcer, and allows histology.
  • Clear, decisive single-stage plan (RYGB + HH repair + cholecystectomy) with sound reasons (post-RYGB gallstone complications are hard to treat).
  • Most process-disciplined: defined pre-op lab panel, mandatory anaesthesia fitness teleclinic, named stapler platform, states he is the primary surgeon.
  • Correctly frames the ulcer as bile/ischaemia-driven and defers H. pylori to long-term GI care rather than delaying surgery.
  • Accessible (same region, visa support, structured coordination).

Cons

  • Did not commit limb lengths in writing — the single most important variable for bile control was left to the consult.
  • "~30 revisions, no complications" is a mixed revision figure, not OAGB→RYGB-specific; "no complications" across all revisions is an optimistic claim that invites scrutiny.
  • Preferred to move discussion off email to teleclinic — reasonable clinically, but left several of the patient's written questions unanswered on paper.
  • A new anastomosis carries a real (if small) leak risk that Lee's approach avoids.

Dr Murat Üstün — RYGB conversion (or reversal), ulcer managed intra-operatively, combined where safe

Pros

  • Most careful, safety-first assessment on paper: the only one to flag the discrepancy in the medication records, insist the ulcer "should not be assumed bile-only," and keep reversal genuinely on the table.
  • Most complete pre-operative pathway: nutrition panel, anaesthetic/cardiac review, specific planning for methylphenidate/hypersomnia, and colonoscopy-lesion follow-up.
  • Explicitly offers a staged option if bleeding/anaemia/nutrition make one-stage unsafe — the most individualised risk posture.
  • Transparent, itemised package pricing and realistic ~10-day timeline including fit-to-fly.
  • Appropriately cautious, hedged language — avoids over-promising.

Cons

  • Least committal surgical plan — much is deferred to "intra-operative assessment," so the patient can't compare a concrete technique.
  • No specific limb length, no firm decision on resect-vs-keep, no stated vagotomy position.
  • Did not state personal/team case volumes for OAGB revision in writing even when directly asked — a gap given the question was explicit.
  • The clinical content arrives wrapped in a commercial "luxury package / special price" frame, which can blur the line between clinical advice and sales.

Dr Fakhrul Islam — advisory opinion: RYGB with a new anastomosis, specified limbs, no vagotomy, remove gallbladder

Pros

  • Clear, decisive and well-reasoned on the two pivotal points: bile — not acid — is the driver, so convert properly and do not do a vagotomy (a view well supported in current practice).
  • The only opinion to commit specific, bile-protective numbers: AL 75 cm + BP 50–60 cm with a refashioned anastomosis.
  • Firm on removing the gallbladder in the same sitting and on at least a cruroplasty for the hiatus.
  • Adds genuine value as a trusted, candid overview that reconciles the conflicting opinions — and honestly flags the patient's information-overload/anxiety as a decision risk.

Cons

  • It is an advisory second opinion, not an operative offer — he would refer the patient on (suggests the USA), so there is no accountable surgeon, cost, or facility attached.
  • No case-volume, no pre-op/anaesthetic work-up detail, no complication data — outside the scope of an advisory note.
  • Asks the patient to "avoid highlighting all the risks" and to trust the plan — reassuring in intent, but under-weights informed consent for a high-complexity revision.
  • Recommendations can't be executed as-is; they must be handed to an operating team, reintroducing the very variability he describes.

How to read these scores. They are close on purpose — all four converge on the right core operation (RYGB conversion). The gaps come from the details: Usama and Fakhrul make the technically strongest ulcer/bile choices; Murat gives the safest work-up; Lee offers the strongest experience and the gentlest operation but the most debatable ulcer decision. A reasonable reading is that the best plan may combine elements: Usama's/Fakhrul's resection + bile-protective limb lengths, delivered with Murat's peri-operative rigour — a point worth raising directly with whichever surgeon the patient chooses.

5. Suggested questions to settle before deciding

  • Resect or keep the ulcer? Ask each surgeon to justify, and ask specifically about biopsy to exclude malignancy in a chronic, bled ulcer.
  • Exact limb lengths (alimentary + biliopancreatic + common channel) and the evidence for the bile-reflux target.
  • Vagotomy — yes or no? If yes, what is the plan for post-vagotomy diarrhoea/stasis, and why is it needed once bile is diverted?
  • Procedure-specific outcomes: leak, bleed, re-operation and mortality rates for OAGB→RYGB specifically (not mixed revisions).
  • Peri-operative optimisation: anaemia correction, anaesthetic plan around methylphenidate/modafinil, H. pylori confirmation, and whether the ulcer must heal first.
  • One-stage vs staged given the recent bleed — and the fall-back if intra-operative findings change the plan.
